Artificial two-body relaxation and force softening appear to be driving the persistent core formation in our halo simulations.  Studies of high-resolution CDM halos show that even **10^6–10^8 particles** have relaxation times short enough to flatten cusps in a few dynamical times.  In particular, **Diemand et al. (2004)** demonstrated that simulated halos suffer central heating that declines only ~N^–0.25 with particle number, not N^–1, implying that **large N** (≫10^8) or softening are needed to prevent cusp erosion.  Similarly, **El-Zant (2006)** finds t_relax∝r^2 and that a 10^6–particle halo is already “noise-dominated” in the inner ~1%, requiring N≫10^8 for the inner profile to remain collisionless.  These works predict that **truncating outer mass** alone cannot halt inner relaxation: removing long-range forces beyond ~10–20 kpc still leaves the small-N central cusp relaxationally unstable.  In contrast, **softening length** must be large enough to suppress close-encounter scattering.  Athanassoula et al. (2000) show there is an “optimal” ε∼N^–0.3: too little softening makes forces noisy (amplifying collisional heating) while too much biases the force (producing an artificial core).  Our runs with the smallest ε show an over-concentrated core (as expected for **under-softening**), whereas large ε yields a spurious core.  In short, the balance of evidence is that **numerical discreteness** (two-body scattering and shot-noise) – not e.g. algorithmic bias or mis-centering – is the culprit.

**Conclusion:**  In summary, the **persistent inner decline** in our collisionless halo is most naturally explained by residual numerical heating.  Both theory and simulations indicate that finite-N **two-body relaxation (and related shot-noise modes)** should secularly flatten cuspy profiles unless N is astronomically large or ε is very coarse.  Our tests (force truncation, softening scans, timestepping) largely rule out algorithmic bugs or centering bias, leaving discreteness-induced diffusion as the leading culprit.  Notably, **removing long-range forces** had only a small impact until we cut to scales ~ε, in line with these papers’ predictions.  Therefore we conclude the decline is a numerical artifact of collisional relaxation: we expect it to weaken only as N→∞ or ε grows, consistent with standard relaxation scalings.
