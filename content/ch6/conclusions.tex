\chapter{Conclusions}
This thesis has investigated the origin of the persistent decline of the inner density observed in collisionless dark matter halos evolved in isolation for 100~Gyr using the SWIFT code. The central conclusion of this work is that the continuous reduction of the inner density in these simulated halos is not a physical collisionless phenomenon, nor is it the result of post-processing errors or algorithmic biases within the gravity solver. Instead, the persistent inner decline is most naturally explained by residual numerical heating; specifically, finite-$N$ two-body relaxation and discreteness noise within the innermost regions of the halo.

The elimination of explanations was achieved through a set of controlled numerical experiments, each designed to isolate a specific aspect of the simulation environment. Across the full set of tests, the simulated core continued to evolve and expand after its initial formation, rather than settling into a stable inner configuration. 

Crucially, this decline proved to be remarkably robust against variations in the numerical and structural setup. Variations in the simulation box size ruled out periodic boundary artefacts and large-scale tidal influences. Modifications to the self-gravity solver parameters demonstrated that the observed density reduction is not a byproduct of approximate force evaluation errors. Furthermore, experiments with different gravitational softening lengths revealed that, while the softening scale alters the detailed rate of the density evolution, it does not prevent the decline. Even when the simulation was initialized with a cored density profile rather than a standard Navarro--Frenk--White (NFW) cusp, the inner density continued to drop, indicating that the mechanism is an ongoing process rather than a one-time adjustment to a specific initial density gradient.

To further isolate the source of the dynamical evolution, a hybrid truncated-NFW model was developed and deployed. This approach allowed assessment of whether the inner density profile is shaped by global potential fluctuations from the outer halo. The hybrid experiments revealed that removing long range forces had only a minimal impact on the density evolution until the truncation scale approached the softening length. This confirms that the driving mechanism of the density decline is highly localized to the central regions. The kinematic diagnostics definitively identify this localized mechanism as numerical heating, as the observed rise in central velocity dispersion matches theoretical expectations for continuous kinetic energy injection via two-body relaxation.

\section{Future work}
Building upon these findings, several directions for future research can be pursued. Since the observed numerical heating is fundamentally a finite-$N$ effect, future studies should execute very high resolution simulations with massively increased particle counts. This approach is necessary to empirically determine the threshold at which two-body relaxation becomes negligible over both cosmological and extended timescales.

Additionally, investigating the implementation of adaptive gravitational softening or alternative softening kernel shapes within SWIFT could provide insights into better suppressing short range noise without compromising the accuracy of the global potential.

A more immediate and practical improvement is related to the hybrid model simulations used in this study. Future work should focus on finding a more systematic method to obtain a smoother potential distribution when coupling the analytical external potential with the inner $N$-body system, moving away from the current reliance on trial-and-error parameter tinkering.

Several technical limitations of the current implementation must also be addressed. Now that the implementation has been validated and its functionality confirmed, a natural next step is to replace the current hard step cutoff at $R_{\mathrm{cut}}$ with a smooth transition shell or blending function. Furthermore, the masking algorithm could be improved in a way that would allow particles to be continuously evaluated and re-masked or unmasked as they migrate across the boundary, while also extending the coupling logic to other particle types. Additionally, to prevent mismatches caused by the drift of the inner particle distribution, future iterations should incorporate dynamic tracking of the system's centre rather than relying on a fixed external potential origin.

Finally, future work should introduce gas dynamics, star formation, and stellar feedback into the simulations. Baryonic processes can lead to physical core formation (e.g., via rapid supernova feedback) or central contraction. Understanding exactly how baseline numerical heating interacts with these physical baryonic effects is crucial for the reliability of modern cosmological hydrodynamical simulations.
